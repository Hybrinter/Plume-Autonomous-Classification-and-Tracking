# Single-axis gimbal tracking time

TEMPORARY ANALYSIS. Generated by `analyze.py`. Not flight software.

## Locked assumptions

From the 2026-09-01 follow-up:

- Elevation window is **one-sided** 90→30 deg. Hard gimbal stops for
  clearance; tracking time stops at the limit, with no leftover FOV walk-out.
- Mount is geocentric nadir. A ≤5 deg offset is a later correction, not in
  this run.
- At most **one plant cluster** per track. Elevation follows the cluster
  CoG. Science is satisfied if a plume is **anywhere in the frame**, so the
  success metric is a covering disk of radius R around that CoG, not
  boresight centering.
- If plants are spread farther than the chip (~±12 km at nadir), CoG is
  the wrong metric: pick the nearest/brightest cluster and treat it as
  its own origin. Do not average across a city.
- Current ISS TLE (Celestrak GP, epoch 2026-09-01). Earth rotation and
  51.63 deg inclination are on. Design pass is **max latitude** (heading
  due east), which is the polar-most nadir ISS can reach.
- FOV is the **most restrictive**: IMX264 active area, then shrunk by the
  lens max distortion of 0.66 %. The 8.8 mm 2/3" sheet HFOV of 3.36 deg
  is larger than this camera and is not used.
- Full-frame inference = the full 2×2 band plane. That does not change
  optical FOV; it is a compute note.
- A ~2028 launch still sits in the current 416–423 km operational band.
  ISS is committed through 2030; retrograde deorbit lowering is a
  2028–2030 lead-up, not a 2028 operations baseline.

## Earth rotation vs pass latitude

At low latitude the ISS heading has a large north component, so Earth
rotation (always east) has a **cross-track** piece. That walks even a
nadir-centered origin in azimuth, peaking at the 30 deg stop where range
is long. At max latitude the heading is due east and the walk vanishes.
That is why a polar-slice one-axis placement is the right one-axis case.

For a point origin, Earth rotation walks it out of the chip below ~40 deg
latitude. For a 5 km cluster the threshold is ~45 deg. Below that,
one-axis only keeps the last tens of seconds near nadir. That is a
2-axis problem, or a choice not to work those passes.

## Plant-cluster covering disk (design pass)

Elevation tracks the cluster CoG. A plume is acquired if it is in the
frame. The covering radius is `R_cover = D + r` for stack half-span D
and per-plume CoG radius r. Equal-weight centroid tracking does *not*
grow with plume count: `R_centroid = r`. The covering disk is the
stricter (and right) metric if we want every plume in the cluster.

Design R = 5 km: a large integrated complex (~4 km wide, e.g. Baytown
refinery) plus a few km of plume CoG. Typical small plants are 1–2 km.

## Full-frame inference

Optical FOV is the full chip either way. Full-frame inference changes
compute, not tracking time. The 2×2 band plane is
**1224 × 1024**.

| | 256×256 (today) | full band plane | scale |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| Pixels | 65536 | 1253376 | 19.1× |
| DilateNet-w32 FLOPs | 0.21 G | 4.02 G | 19.1× |
| ShuffleNet FLOPs | 0.110 G | 2.10 G | 19.1× |
| CPU accept latency (linear scale from 256) | 2.3 / 3.4 ms | 44 / 65 ms | |

The 500× parameter cut (DilateNet-w32 23.5 k vs U-Net 13.4 M) is what
makes full-frame plausible. FLOPs still fit the 500 ms budget if latency
scales with pixels. Jetson Nano (4 GB) is tighter on **activation memory**
at 1224×1024 than Orin NX; that needs a bench, not a FLOP argument.
A 256-pixel centre crop on this optic is only ~0.67 deg — that would
throw away the 1-axis lateral budget. Full-frame is the right inference
size for this lens.

## Bottom line

1. Along-track, one elevation axis gives **124 s**
   at the design (max-lat) pass, vs **3.0 s** staring.
   Stop at 30 deg. Peak rate is under 2 deg/s.
2. At that pass, Earth-rotation az walk of the origin is
   **0.007 deg**. A 5 km plant cluster stays in the
   ±1.60 deg chip for the whole window — **0 s lost**
   vs two-axis.
3. At equatorial and mid-latitude passes, Earth rotation walks the
   origin out at the 30 deg stop. One-axis then keeps only the last
   ~20–60 s depending on cluster size. Use two-axis for those passes,
   or do not work them. From ~45 deg up, a 5 km cluster is lossless.
4. If two plants are farther apart than ~24 km, they are two clusters.
   Track one.
